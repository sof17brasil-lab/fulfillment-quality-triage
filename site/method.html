How to reproduce it

Four commands. The last one must end with ALL CHECKS PASSED.

pip install -r requirements.txt
python scripts/load.py            # CSVs -> build/olist.duckdb, checks row counts
python scripts/run.py             # runs sql/*.sql in order -> out/*.json
python scripts/verify_pandas.py   # independent recomputation -> out/pandas_recheck.json
python scripts/check.py           # must end with ALL CHECKS PASSED

The raw CSVs are not in this repository. data/README.md says where to get them. scripts/build_site.py copies the results into site/data/, which is the only thing the page reads.

Definitions

Outcome: every order lands in exactly one bucket

Delivered on time
Delivered before the end of the promised calendar day.
Delivered late
Delivered, but after the end of the promised day.
Cancelled
Status canceled or unavailable.
Stalled
Paid for and never delivered by the time the data ends.

The promise is a calendar date, so the deadline is the end of that day. Treating it as midnight at the start would mark a large number of same-day arrivals late.

Late cause: a counterfactual, not a guess

For every late order we ask one question: if the seller had handed the parcel over exactly at the shipping deadline, would it still have been late?

Seller handoff
The handoff was late, and the delay was at least as large as the lateness. An on-time handoff alone would have saved it.
Shared
The handoff was late, but the parcel was so slow in transit that an on-time handoff would not have been enough.
Carrier or promise
The parcel left on time and still arrived late.
Unknown
No handoff timestamp. One order in the whole window.

The monitor: a p-chart, not a trend line

The expected rate for a week is the median of the prior 26 weeks, so one bad month cannot drag the baseline up and hide the next one. The upper control limit is expected plus three standard errors for that week’s volume. SEV1 means more than double that limit — a deliberately high bar, which is why only 8 of 86 weeks clear it.

Seller flagging: Wilson lower bound

A seller with 3 late handoffs out of 10 is not provably worse than the platform. Each seller gets a Wilson 95% lower bound — the lowest late-handoff rate still plausible given their volume — and is flagged only when even that bound sits above the platform rate. Ranking on the raw rate would put low-volume sellers at the top by chance.

Month-matching, and why 148 ÷ 39,516 is not the number on the page

Cancellation rates fell across the whole platform over the window, and veteran-seller orders skew later in time, so a raw comparison of new against veteran sellers mostly measures the calendar. The launch scorecard therefore compares new sellers only against veterans in the same calendar month, then combines months weighted by how many new-seller orders each month held.

That weighted figure is 0.39%. The unweighted ratio of the two totals printed beside it is 0.37%. The difference is the weighting, and the weighting is the entire point of the control, so the page labels it a month-matched rate rather than implying a division. The same stratified method is used for the repeat-purchase check in query 08.

The ledger

Every number shown anywhere on this site has an id here, a claim in plain words, the query it came from, and — where an independent recomputation exists — the pandas value it was checked against. scripts/verify_pandas.py recomputes the headline figures straight from the raw CSVs and shares no logic with the SQL, so a bug would have to exist twice, written two different ways, to survive.

Leads that were tested and dropped

A real-time early warning — dropped

The late rate for a purchase week is only known about three weeks later, once those orders are due. A count of parcels stuck in transit more than 10 days, taken at each week’s end, might fire sooner. It does not. In the week before each episode’s first SEV1 the signal sat inside its own normal range — 35.1% before Black Friday and 41.5% before February–March, both “ok”. It gave no usable lead time, so it is not shown as a fix. sql/12_early_warning.sql keeps the test.

New sellers cancel more — survived, and moved to the page

The first look showed new sellers cancelling far more than veterans, which looked like a straightforward launch-quality problem. It was partly the calendar. Tested within matched months the gap narrows but holds, so it earned a place on the main page with its counts shown.

Complaint themes — not attempted

10,721 of the low-star reviews carry written text in Portuguese. Sorting them into defect reasons would need a hand-labelled sample to measure accuracy, and that work has not been done, so no claim is made about what customers wrote.

Limits

  1. This is multi-day parcel shipping, not same-day grocery. The methods transfer. The timescales and the specific numbers do not.
  2. There are no reschedule events and no shopper-supply data, so two of the defect types a fulfilment quality team tracks cannot be shown at all.
  3. Fault attribution uses timestamps. A seller who hands off late because the carrier missed a pickup is blamed unfairly. With real operations data you would check pickup scans.
  4. The data ends in 2018 and covers one marketplace in one country.
  5. The repeat-purchase result is observational and small — 35 and 41 repeat customers. It suggests a direction. A company with frequent reorders could measure it properly in a day.
  6. The monitor looks back at purchase weeks. A live version would need an early-warning signal, and the one tested here did not work.
  7. No Instacart internal number, cost, defect rate, team or tool appears anywhere in this project. None are public. The calculator uses the reader’s own inputs.
